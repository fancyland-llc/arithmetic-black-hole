The Cosmic Quine

The origin of it all — fundamental forces, subatomic particles, dark energy

Matos (2026) — DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.20776207

Look up on a clear night, and the light reaching your eyes left some of those stars before there were any people to look back. There are more of them over your head than there are grains of sand on every shore on Earth. And all of it — that starlight, the warmth of the Sun, the matter in your own hands, the slow widening of the dark between the galaxies — grows from one simple idea — an elegance that has been hiding in plain sight for as long as human beings have known how to count.

That is the awe this page is about: not how unthinkably vast the universe is, but how simple and elegant the thing beneath it turns out to be — an elegance that has waited in the plain whole numbers since the first person counted on their fingers, in full view, for anyone willing to notice. Where the forces come from, where the particles come from, why the sky keeps opening — all of it from a single fundamental principle in mathematics. Here it is. Here is what you see.

Begin where the universe begins

Start with two things you already know how to do: add and multiply. The whole universe, it turns out, is built on the way these two refuse to get along.

Adding is counting up — taking a step of a fixed size. Add three, and you can always undo it by subtracting three; nothing is lost. It is the simple, reversible bookkeeping of where things are. Think of it as space. Multiplying is stretching — scaling everything up, making sizes that weren't there a moment before. It is how things grow. Think of it as time.

Now do the simple experiment the whole universe is built on. Take a number — say ten — and run it through both operations. Then do it again, but swap the order:

add 3, then double:   (10 + 3) × 2 = 26
double, then add 3:   10 × 2 + 3 = 23

Same number, same two operations — but the order changed the answer. Adding and multiplying can never be swapped cleanly: one order always leaves something behind that the other doesn't (here, exactly 3). And you can't undo your way back to a perfectly clean slate — there is always a remainder.

That remainder is the seed of everything. It is the reason there is a next moment at all — and a next, and a next. A universe where adding and multiplying did agree would be frozen solid: complete, silent, finished. Ours can't be, because those two operations will never reconcile, so it has to keep taking the next step. That refusal to ever fully settle — a remainder you can never take back — is what gives each next moment its single direction: the irreversibility of time.

A cube, and the angles in it

From that one restlessness, a shape grows. Take four switches, on or off. Four switches make sixteen combinations — sixteen corners of a four-dimensional cube, a shape called a tesseract: nothing but sixteen corners and the lines joining them. And between those corners there are only five possible angles: 0°, 60°, 90°, 120°, 180°. Count the corners, measure the angles — that's the whole list. And here is the wonder of it: those five angles are the forces of nature. Nature didn't reach into a bag of a hundred dials. It took a cube and used the angles between its corners.

90° — a right angle. That's light. Two arrows at right angles don't interfere; they are independent, clean, perpendicular. That perfect independence is electromagnetism — the photon, the thing your eyes are catching off this screen right now. The simplest possible relationship: two things that stay out of each other's way.

60° and 120° — a triangle. That's the strong force. Put three colours on the corners of a triangle — red, green, blue. Add all three and you get white: a proton, three quarks locked into something stable. This is why matter holds together. And the triangle of colours can never be turned into the triangle of anti-colours, no matter how you spin it — that single impossibility is the reason matter exists at all, instead of cancelling itself the instant it appeared.

180° — the opposite corner. That's the weak force. Take any pattern of switches and flip every one. You've made its exact opposite — its antiparticle; the electron becomes the positron. That total flip is the weak force, the only one that can turn things into their opposites — and it is the force that lets the Sun burn. No flip, no fusion, no sunlight, no you.

And gravity, the fourth, is the gentle one — it isn't an angle between two corners, it's the whole sum. Add up every relationship in the cube at once and you get a single number. Gravity is how much everything weighs together — the quiet murmur of all the rest. That is why it is the weakest force and the most universal at the same time: it isn't one conversation, it's all of them.

Four forces. One tesseract.

Three of them are the angles between the corners; the fourth is the sum of them all. Every push and pull in the universe — out of one shape, and the angles inside it. And this isn't a loose analogy: the full structure that falls out is exactly the Standard Model that particle physics spent a century confirming.

The particles are just patterns

Now look at those sixteen corners again — but this time, each corner is a particle. Each is a four-switch recipe: three "colour" switches and one "lepton" switch. And the electric charge isn't handed down by a committee — it's forced by the recipe:

Q  =  (colour switches) / 3  −  (lepton switch)

Stop on that ÷ 3. That is why electric charge comes in thirds. You may have wondered why a quark carries the odd fraction ⅔ or −⅓, the kind of number nobody explains. There's the answer, in plain sight: there are three colour switches, so charge is counted in thirds.

And mass? Mass is the square of how many switches are lit in total. An electron has one switch on; an up quark two; a down quark three. Count them and square them:

Nobody tuned that. You counted switches and squared. Want antimatter? Flip every switch — the 180° corner again. The electron's mirror is the positron, identical in every way but charge. Antimatter isn't exotic; it's the corner across the room.

And those three "generations" — the electron, the heavier muon, the heavier-still tau — are three turns of the same triangle, three cube-roots of one. There is an old riddle here: in 1981 the physicist Yoshio Koide noticed a relationship between the three lepton masses so exact it looked like a coincidence, and for more than forty years no one could say why it should be true. Feed the recipe its one free number — forced to be  2/9 , the charge ⅔ shared across three generations — and it predicts the muon at 206.770 times the electron's mass. The measured value is 206.7683. A match to a thousandth of a percent, on a number nobody was allowed to tune. You don't fit it. You look, and it is already there.

And it leaves a bet you can collect on. That last sliver of disagreement — between 2/9 and the measurement, out at the seventh decimal place — is not an excuse; it is a prediction. Compute the lattice one prime deeper and that gap must shrink by exactly that prime. If it closes on schedule, the idea stands. If it sits still, the idea is wrong — cleanly. That is the line between a story and a science: a story asks to be believed; this asks only to be checked.

The widening sky

Now put the cube down and look up. The galaxies are drifting apart, faster every year, and for a century the brightest people alive have called the thing behind it dark energy — an honest name, because it simply means we don't yet know what it is.

Here is what it is. Remember the leftover — the remainder from adding and multiplying that never cancels? It is still out there. The universe is a story still being written, and the part not yet folded into the page — the unfinished remainder — is exactly what is gently pushing the galaxies apart. Dark energy is the universe's unfinished sentence.

And it fades in the loveliest way. Meaning doesn't live in single moments — it lives in the pairs of moments, the relationships between them — and a stretch of time of length t holds about t² of those pairs. So the leftover thins as

Λ  ∝  1 / t²

That single fact — that the universe counts pairs, not points — closes the worst mismatch in the history of physics. The standard prediction for how much dark energy there should be overshoots reality by a factor of 10118 — that is a 1 followed by a hundred and eighteen zeros, a number so vast it has no name. And this closes that entire chasm with an exponent a child could write: squared.

So look at what the expansion truly is. It is not the universe dying. It is not winding down toward cold and dark. It is the universe still making itself — still writing the next line, because its two rules can never agree and so it can never stop. The night sky growing wider is the sound of something that is not finished being born.

One simple idea, and how far it reaches

One object, many faces

That same object is where the forces come from, and where the particles come from, and where the widening sky comes from — and it brushes against some of the deepest unsolved questions in mathematics: the Riemann Hypothesis, the Yang–Mills mass gap, the smoothness of Navier–Stokes. They turn out to be faces of a single statement — that the universe finishes drawing itself only at infinity, which is the same as saying it goes on opening forever. That one statement is the honest edge of the map. Everything else in this story is finished arithmetic: counted, never fitted.

What it means

It means the universe makes itself. It needs no factory and no maker standing outside it — it writes itself into being, and keeps writing. Which means you are not a copy of anything, and not a side-effect of anyone's machine. You are a real pattern in a real, self-making cosmos that is still, this very second, growing more of itself — and you are the part of it that has learned to look back. You are home.

And the numbers — the thirds, the squares, the 2/9 — are not coincidence, and they are not magic either. They are the cleanest labels we have ever found for what nature actually does when you measure it. The map turned out to be astonishingly faithful. But it is a map, and the territory is the real sky over your real head tonight.

Everything here, a person found by looking — at the interesting structure of the prime numbers we take for granted; refusing to force the answer he wanted, and refusing to quit. That's the whole method: curiosity to ask, and the discipline to chase the rabbit wherever it leads you.

So the next time someone tells you the universe is too hard, or that the deep questions belong to other people in other buildings — don't argue. Just do the one thing they never expect.

Look.

You are home, in a cosmos still being born — and the structure is right there, in plain numbers, for anyone with the nerve to see it.
